    SMI26 · instructions as art

    The idea is the machine.

    Sol LeWitt never drew most of his wall drawings. He wrote them. A few sentences on a certificate, handed to whoever was on the ladder that week. The sentences are the artwork. Every drawing made from them is a performance.

    The idea becomes a machine that makes the art.Sol LeWitt, Paragraphs on Conceptual Art, Artforum, 1967

    LeWitt (1928 to 2007) made his first wall drawing in 1968 at the Paula Cooper Gallery in New York, and more than twelve hundred after it. When a museum buys one, it buys a certificate and a diagram, not a wall. The drawing can be painted over and drawn again somewhere else, by different hands, and it is still the same work. Today, assistants and art students still draw them, following the words.

    That is exactly what you do when you write a program. You write the rule; something else carries it out. LeWitt's drafters were people, so his rules could be loose. Yours is a computer, so every loose word has to become a decision. This module is about that gap: between an idea you can say in a sentence and a procedure that runs, and how to fill it with something that is yours.

    One instruction, six drafters

    Here is a paraphrase of one of his most famous instructions, Wall Drawing #118 (1971): on a wall, place fifty points at random, evenly spread; connect every point to every other point with a straight pencil line. Below, six drafters follow it. Each has their own hand and their own idea of "random".

    The idea that matters: an instruction is a set of decisions plus a set of freedoms. LeWitt decided the points, the number, the connections. He left free where exactly the points go and how a human hand wobbles. Designing an algorithm is choosing which is which.

    • Set "evenly" to pure random and look for clumps and holes. Then keep apart. Which one is what LeWitt meant? (We don't know. Neither did his drafters.)
    • Drop the points to 6. Is it still the same artwork? At what number does it stop feeling like #118?
    • Look at drafter E, "a machine". Is the drawing better or worse without the wobble? Why?
    chapter 1

    Be the drafter

    Before you write an algorithm, follow one. Your hand is the computer here, and the instruction is the judge. You will find out fast which words are clear and which only feel clear.

    LeWitt's instructions use a small, plain vocabulary, and nearly every one has the same parts:

    partthe question it answersLeWitt's wordsin code
    spacewhere?"on a wall", "a 6-foot square", "a grid of 12-inch squares"createCanvas(), a loop over cells
    elementwhat mark?"straight lines", "not straight lines", "broken lines", "arcs"line(), curveVertex(), arc()
    placementfrom where to where?"from the corners", "from the midpoints of the sides", "to points on a grid"coordinates, random()
    quantityhow many?"fifty", "ten thousand", "as many as possible"for loops, while conditions
    constraintwhat's forbidden?"not touching", "evenly", "within the circle"if, rejection, clipping
    systemhow do the parts relate?"all combinations", "superimposed", "each part different"nested loops, bitmasks, recursion

    Now be the drafter. Pick an instruction, draw it with your mouse or finger, and let the page judge you. Then press machine's turn and judge the machine.

    • Try the third instruction. It doesn't say how many lines. Who decided "at least 8"? Was that fair?
    • Find a drawing that you think follows the instruction but the judge rejects. Then one the judge accepts that you think is wrong. Both are bugs in the translation from words to rules.
    • Watch how the machine solves "not touching": it throws a line down, checks it against every other, and throws it away if they touch. That's an algorithm called rejection sampling, and it is completely unlike how your hand did it. Two procedures, same instruction.

    Vera Molnar called this the machine imaginaire. From 1959, years before she could use a computer, she made paintings by writing simple programs and executing them by hand, step by step, as if she were the machine. When she got to a real computer in 1968, she already knew how to think like one.

    chapter 2

    All combinations

    LeWitt's favourite way to make an idea big was to refuse to choose. Not "pick the best two kinds of line", but "draw every combination of them, in order". The system does the composing.

    In 1969 he published a small book called Four Basic Kinds of Straight Lines and Their Combinations: vertical, horizontal, and the two diagonals. One of each, then every pair, then every three, then all four superimposed. Fifteen drawings. You don't need taste to decide what goes in the series; you need arithmetic. In Wall Drawing #260 (1975) he did the same with white lines on a black wall: all two-part combinations of arcs from corners and sides, straight, not straight and broken lines.

    The code for "every combination" is one of the most useful tricks in this whole course. Count from 1 to 15 in binary and read the four bits as four switches:

    For each number from 1 to 15:
  if its first bit is on, draw vertical lines
  if its second bit is on, draw horizontal lines
  if its third bit is on, draw diagonal-right lines
  if its fourth bit is on, draw diagonal-left lines
    for (let k = 1; k < 16; k++) {      // 0001 .. 1111
  if (k & 1) drawVertical();         // bit 1
  if (k & 2) drawHorizontal();       // bit 2
  if (k & 4) drawDiagonalRight();    // bit 3
  if (k & 8) drawDiagonalLeft();     // bit 4
}
    for k in range(1, 16):
    if k & 1: draw_vertical()
    if k & 2: draw_horizontal()
    if k & 4: draw_diagonal_right()
    if k & 8: draw_diagonal_left()
    • Choose 3 kinds of line and "every combination". Count the squares before you look. (2³ − 1 = 7.)
    • Turn on colour. LeWitt often gave each direction its own colour so that superimposed squares mix: yellow and blue make green where they cross.
    • Pick 6 kinds and "pairs". That's 15 drawings again, but a completely different series. The rule for the series is a creative choice as big as the lines themselves.

    Why this matters for inventing: permutation is a way to escape your own taste. You choose a small set of elements, and then the system forces you to look at combinations you would never have picked. Some of them will be the best things in the series.

    chapter 3

    Words into code

    Translating an instruction is mostly translating its nouns into shapes and its adjectives into decisions. The verbs are easy. The adjectives are where the art goes.

    the wordsone possible codethe decision you just made
    "fifty points"for (let i = 0; i < 50; i++)a constant. Or should it be a slider?
    "at random"random(width), random(height)uniform, every point equally likely
    "evenly spread"a grid with jitter, or best-candidate samplingwhich "even"? (chapter 0, demo above)
    "every point to every other"for i … for j = i + 1 …each pair once, not twice
    "not straight"noise() displacement along the linehow far it strays, how fast it wiggles
    "not touching"check and reject, or design so it can't happenrejection is general; design is elegant
    "from the corners"[0,0], [width,0], [width,height], [0,height]which corner first, if order matters
    "all combinations"count 1 to 2ⁿ − 1, read the bitsthe order of the series
    • In the last sketch, change x * 0.004 to x * 0.05. Lines start to touch. Why? (Neighbours now read very different noise.) Find the largest value where they still never touch.
    • In the first sketch, switch between the two presets and count the lines. Same number. Different artwork?
    • In the bitmask sketch, make it 5 directions (add one at a different angle). How many squares now, and what size grid do you need?

    Casey Reas (co-creator of Processing) did this translation as an artwork: {Software} Structures (Whitney Artport, 2004) implements three LeWitt wall drawings in software, then writes new instructions of Reas's own, each interpreted by several different programmers. Read the essay alongside the code: it is the best short text on this topic.

    chapter 4

    The invention machine

    LeWitt's instructions are built from a small grammar: a space, a mark, a placement, a number, a colour, a system. Pull those apart into cards, deal them, and you get instructions nobody has written. Some are nonsense. Some are better than what you would have thought of.

    Each card is one decision. Change any card or deal a fresh hand. The page writes the instruction in LeWitt's dry style, gives it a number, and has a drafter execute it. When your cards contradict each other, the drafter has to improvise, and it tells you so in red.

    • Deal ten hands without changing anything. Copy the two instructions you like best. What do they have in common? That's your taste showing.
    • Lock everything, press "new drafter" five times. Is it the same artwork every time? (The certificate number doesn't change. LeWitt would say yes.)
    • Find a hand that produces a red note. Rewrite the instruction yourself, in your own words, so that the drafter doesn't have to guess.
    • Find what the machine can't say. What would you add as an eighth card? "Lines that avoid each other"? "Each mark half the size of the last"? Write the options for it. That card is the start of your own algorithm.

    A warning. This machine is a grammar of LeWitt's ideas, so it can only make LeWitt-ish things. Dealing cards is a way to warm up, not a way to be original. Originality starts when you invent a card the deck doesn't have, which is the next chapter.

    chapter 5

    Where rules come from

    "Have an idea" is not advice. Here are six places to dig for one. Each is a kind of rule, not a kind of picture, and each makes a different family of images.

    Six questions that generate algorithms

    sourceask yourselfartists who live here
    PermutationWhat is my small set of parts, and what are all the ways to combine them?Sol LeWitt, Vera Molnar, Manfred Mohr
    AccumulationWhat if each mark depends on the one before it?Agnes Denes, Roman Verostko, every flow field
    RecursionWhat if the rule applies to its own output, smaller?Fractals, L-systems, Tyler Hobbs' subdivisions
    ConstraintWhat is the one thing that may never happen?OuLiPo writers, circle packing, Georges Perec
    TranslationWhat data, text or measurement from my world could become form?Hanne Darboven, Ryoji Ikeda, Refik Anadol
    ChanceWhere should I stop deciding and let a die decide?John Cage, Ellsworth Kelly, François Morellet

    Steal structure, not style

    The richest rules are already lying around, in systems that are not art. Borrow the structure; ignore what it looks like.

    • Kolam. Every morning across South India, threshold drawings are made by rule: a grid of dots, and a single line that loops around them, often never lifting and never ending. Mathematicians have written papers about the grammar of sikku kolam. It is one of the oldest algorithmic drawing traditions alive, and you have probably walked past one this week.
    • Weaving and knitting. A loom is a binary machine: each thread is over or under. Jacquard's punched cards (1804) inspired Babbage's computer. Any weaving draft is a program.
    • Games and sports. The rules of kabaddi, carrom or chess are constraints on movement. Make marks wherever the rules would allow a move.
    • Music scores. Fluxus artists wrote event scores that are just instructions; La Monte Young's Composition 1960 #10 is one line about drawing a line. Yoko Ono's Grapefruit (1964) is a whole book of them.
    • Verbs. In 1967 to 1968 Richard Serra wrote a list of verbs (to roll, to fold, to split, to scatter, and about a hundred more) and then made sculpture by doing them to materials. Pick a verb. Do it to a line. Do it again to the result.
    • Bureaucracy. Forms, timetables, queue systems, traffic rules (see the Traffic sketches in this course). Any system that tells people what to do next is an algorithm in disguise.

    LeWitt also wrote, in his Sentences on Conceptual Art (1969), that "irrational thoughts should be followed absolutely and logically." Your idea is allowed to be strange. Your execution of it should be strict. That combination is where the surprising work comes from.

    chapter 6 · studio

    Your own algorithm

    A method for one studio session, about two hours. You will write an instruction, watch other people fail to follow it, and use their mistakes to design an algorithm that is genuinely yours.

    1. Write (10 min)An instruction for a drawing in under 40 words. Words only, no sketches, no pictures in your head of what it "should" look like. Use at least one source from the last chapter.
    2. Swap (15 min)Give it to two other people. They each draw it by hand on A4, separately, without asking you anything. You may not explain. (LeWitt's drafters had to decide for themselves too.)
    3. Compare (10 min)Lay the drawings side by side. Every difference between them points at an ambiguity in your words. Paste your instruction into the finder below and mark the ones that actually mattered.
    4. Decide (15 min)For each ambiguity, choose one: fix it (give a number), free it (hand it to random()), or expose it (make it a parameter, a slider, a variable at the top of your sketch). This is the real design work.
    5. Code it (40 min)Start from the skeleton below. Get the dumbest version running first. Then add the decisions, one at a time.
    6. Make the series (20 min)Find the one parameter that changes the drawing most. Make every value of it, or every combination, like LeWitt. Print or screenshot them as a grid.
    7. Title itWall Drawing #(your roll number). Write the final instruction under the grid. The text is the work; the drawings are performances of it.

      The finder is deliberately paranoid: it flags every word a drafter, or a computer, would have to interpret. Not every flag is a problem. Some ambiguity is the freedom you want to give away. The point is to decide that on purpose.
