Chapter 3 · 1961 – 2019

Moments that bent the line

A history is not a list of artists. It is also the rooms where people met, the papers that gave them new materials, and the tools that let a painter type. Here are the ones that mattered most — with two of the materials, Life and noise, for you to handle.

  • 1961–1973 · ZAGREBNew Tendencies (Nove tendencije)

    Five exhibitions in a city on the far side of the Iron Curtain, where Western constructivists, kinetic artists and, from 1968, computer artists could meet. The 1968–69 edition, Computers and Visual Research, was the first international show devoted to the subject, with a journal, bit international. Molnár, Mohr, Nake and Nees all passed through.

  • 1965 · NEW YORKThe Responsive Eye, MoMA

    Op art: Bridget Riley, Victor Vasarely, Josef Albers. Not computer art — but pictures made from rules, grids and systematic variation, seen by 180,000 people. It made the audience for what followed.

  • 1968 · LONDONCybernetic Serendipity, ICA

    Curated by Jasia Reichardt. Sixty thousand visitors. Computer poems, drawing machines, Nake, Nees, Noll, Csuri, John Whitney's films, Gordon Pask's Colloquy of Mobiles, a robot by Bruce Lacey, and the first exhibited computer music. The catalogue is still in print. It was the moment the public met the idea.

  • 1968 · UTRECHTAristid Lindenmayer publishes L-systems

    A biologist describes plant growth as string rewriting: start with A, replace A→AB and B→A every generation. Read the string as turtle commands and you get a fern. Every generative tree, branch and coral since owes him.

  • 1970 · CAMBRIDGE, UKJohn Conway's Game of Life

    Published in Martin Gardner's Scientific American column, October 1970. Four rules on a grid. Gliders, guns, and eventually a working computer built inside it. The single most-run program in generative art. Below.

  • 1970 · NEW YORKSoftware, Jewish Museum

    Jack Burnham's exhibition proposed that art was becoming information: instructions, systems, feedback. Half the machines broke. The idea did not.

  • 1975 / 1982Benoît Mandelbrot names the fractal

    The Fractal Geometry of Nature (1982) gave artists a word for the self-similar roughness of coastlines and clouds, and a set — the Mandelbrot set — that a home computer could draw for the first time in 1985. A generation learned to program by rendering it.

  • 1983 · NEW YORKKen Perlin invents noise

    Working on Tron, Perlin wanted textures that looked natural rather than machine-made. He wrote a function that returns smooth, continuous randomness — noise(x, y, z). He got a Technical Academy Award for it in 1997. It is the most-used function in this course. Below.

  • 1983Stephen Wolfram classifies cellular automata

    Rule 30, Rule 110, and the claim that simple programs can be as complex as anything in nature. A New Kind of Science followed in 2002.

  • 1986Craig Reynolds's Boids

    Three rules — separate, align, cohere — and a screen full of dots becomes a flock. Used in Batman Returns (1992) for the bats. Used in KARMA (2023) by your instructor. See chapter 5.

  • 1995The Algorists

    Roman Verostko, Jean-Pierre Hébert and Helaman Ferguson name the movement at SIGGRAPH: artists who write their own algorithms. A manifesto in the form of an if statement.

  • 1999John Maeda, Design By Numbers

    At the MIT Media Lab, a tiny programming language and a book meant for designers, not engineers. Two of his students take the idea further.

  • 2001 · MITProcessing

    Casey Reas and Ben Fry. Free, open-source, one window, a run button, and a sketchbook metaphor. For the first time, an art student could write a generative program in an afternoon. Reas's own work — Process series, Software Structures (2004, a direct homage to LeWitt's instructions) — proved the tool with art.

  • 2003Philip Galanter defines the term

    "Generative art refers to any art practice where the artist uses a system, such as a set of natural language rules, a computer program, a machine, or other procedural invention, which is set into motion with some degree of autonomy contributing to or resulting in a completed work of art." Still the definition most people cite.

  • 2005openFrameworks

    Zach Lieberman, Theo Watson and Arturo Castro: Processing's ideas in C++, for installations that need to run fast. Lieberman's daily sketches, posted for years, are a course in themselves.

  • 2013p5.js

    Lauren Lee McCarthy, with the Processing Foundation: Processing for the browser, in JavaScript, with a community code of conduct written before the first release. This is the tool you are using. Daniel Shiffman's The Coding Train videos, from 2015, are how most of the world learned it.

  • 2019Autoglyphs

    Larva Labs put a generative program on the Ethereum blockchain, so that the chain itself draws the picture. 512 outputs, then the generator turned itself off. Chapter 4 begins here.

1983

Noise

random() gives you a different number every time — jagged, unrelated. noise(x) gives you a number that is close to the one at x − 0.01. That single property — continuity — is why a flow field looks like water and not static. Below, three thousand particles each take sixty steps, and at every step ask noise(x, y) which way to turn.

Try: replace noise(...) with random() in the angle line. That is the whole difference between 1983 and before. Then put noise back and change TWO_PI * 2 to TWO_PI * 8.

Try: RULES = { F: "F[+F]F[-F]F" } with AXIOM = "F", GENERATIONS = 4, ANGLE = 25. Then invent your own rule. Every plant in every generative garden is one of these.
