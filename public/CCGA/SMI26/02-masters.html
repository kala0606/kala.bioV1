Chapter 2 · 1950 – 1975

The masters

An oscilloscope in Iowa. A plotter in Stuttgart. A room at Bell Labs. Then two people in Paris — Vera Molnár and Manfred Mohr — who decided to spend the rest of their lives on it. Every work on this page is a program. Here they are again, as programs.

1950

Ben Laposky and the electronic abstraction

Before there was a computer to draw with, there was an oscilloscope. Ben Laposky (1914–2000), a mathematician and draftsman in Cherokee, Iowa, wired sine-wave generators into a cathode-ray tube and photographed the glowing trace. He called them Oscillons and exhibited them in 1953 as "Electronic Abstractions". They are the first images made by electronic means and shown as art — and they are, precisely, Lissajous curves.

1965

Three N's, three exhibitions, one year

Georg Nees, Frieder Nake, A. Michael Noll. None of them called themselves artists at first. All three showed computer drawings in 1965, and nobody had done that before.

  • February 1965, Stuttgart. Georg Nees, a mathematician at Siemens, shows plotter drawings at the Studiengalerie of the Technische Hochschule, on the invitation of philosopher Max Bense. Bense's booklet for the show is the first text on computer art. At the opening a painter asks Nees whether his machine could make art the way he does. Nees: "No — but it can make what you make." Legend has it half the room walked out.
  • April 1965, New York. Noll and Béla Julesz show at the Howard Wise Gallery. "Computer-Generated Pictures." Reviewed politely, bought by no one.
  • November 1965, Stuttgart. Nees and Nake together at Galerie Wendelin Niedlich. Nake's Hommage à Paul Klee comes out of the Zuse Graphomat here.

Nees's 1969 doctoral thesis, supervised by Bense, was titled Generative Computergrafik. That is where our name comes from.

1924–2023

Vera Molnár

Born Vera Gács in Budapest, 1924. Trained as a painter; moved to Paris in 1947 and stayed for the next 76 years. From 1959 she worked with what she called the machine imaginaire: she would set a rule, then carry it out by hand, exhaustively, on paper — every variation, in order — as if she were a computer. In 1968 she got access to an actual one, at the Sorbonne's computing centre in Orsay, and a Benson plotter. She was 44.

Her subject, for the next fifty-five years, was a single question: how much disorder does the eye want? She called it "1% de désordre". A grid of squares is boring; a pile of squares is noise; somewhere in between there is a place where the picture starts to breathe, and she wanted to find it by measurement, not by taste.

The computer helps, but it does not do the work. It's like a shovel — a wonderful tool, but you have to dig.Vera Molnár

She sold her first work to a museum in her 70s, had her first major retrospective at 88 (Centre Pompidou, 2012), and in 2023, at 99, released a generative NFT series, Thèmes et variations, with Sotheby's. She died in Paris on 7 December 2023, a month before her hundredth birthday.

b. 1938

Manfred Mohr

Born in Pforzheim, Germany, 1938. A jazz saxophonist first, then a painter of hard-edged abstractions in Paris. In 1969, after reading Max Bense and meeting the composer Pierre Barbaud, he taught himself FORTRAN and got night-time access to a plotter at the Paris meteorological institute. From then on, he said, he would let the computer make decisions he could not make himself — and the drawings became more personal, not less.

In 1971 the ARC at the Musée d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris gave him Une Esthétique Programmée — the first solo exhibition of computer-generated art in a museum. Then, from 1973 to 1975, he started on the object he has been taking apart ever since: the cube.

Cubic Limit. A cube has twelve edges. Treat them as an alphabet. Rotate the cube, remove edges according to a rule, and what remains on the page is a sign — a letter from a language that has never been spoken. Mohr made hundreds of these, then films of them, then moved into four, five, six, eleven dimensions (a hypercube in eleven dimensions has 11,264 edges). He is still working, in New York, at 88.

I did not want to be an artist who uses a computer. I wanted to think with it.Manfred Mohr

ALSO

Names you should know

Harold Cohen1928–2016 · AARON, from 1973

Painter who spent forty years teaching a program to draw and paint. AARON knew about arms, plants, and perspective. Cohen: "The most interesting thing I ever did was to make something that could surprise me."

Lillian Schwartz1927–2024 · Bell Labs, from 1968

Films — Pixillation (1970), Mutations — that were the first computer-generated art shown at MoMA. Also proved, with a computer, that the Mona Lisa's face matches Leonardo's self-portrait.

Herbert W. Franke1927–2022 · Vienna / Munich

Physicist, science-fiction novelist, and the one who wrote the first history of the field (Computer Graphics – Computer Art, 1971) while it was still happening. Kept working into his 90s; released NFTs in 2022.

Hiroshi Kawano1925–2012 · Tokyo

Philosopher who made Markov-chain paintings on an OKITAC 5090 from 1964 — before the three N's, but unknown in the West until ZKM rediscovered him.

Roman Verostko1929–2024 · Minneapolis

Former Benedictine monk; plotter drawings with brushes taped to the pen. In 1995 he named the tribe: Algorists. "If (creation && object of art && algorithm && one's own algorithm) { include * an algorist * }."

Colette & Charles Bangertfrom 1967 · Kansas

She painted; he programmed; they signed jointly. Plotter drawings of grasses and fields — the first generative landscapes.

John Whitney1917–1995 · Los Angeles

Repurposed a WWII anti-aircraft gun director into a motion-control camera. The title sequence of Hitchcock's Vertigo (1958) is Lissajous curves. Arabesque (1975) is the first computer film that is unmistakably beautiful.

Charles Csuri1922–2022 · Ohio State

Sine Curve Man (1967), Hummingbird (1967, animated, bought by MoMA). Built the lab where much of computer animation was invented.
