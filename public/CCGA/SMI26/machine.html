    SMI26 · how computers work

    From sand to sites.

    The laptop in front of you is a pile of purified sand, some copper, and a lot of ideas stacked on top of each other. Each idea is simple. The trick is that there are eleven of them, and each one only has to understand the one below.

    When you write circle(200, 200, 50) in p5, you are standing at the top of a very tall tower. That one line becomes JavaScript, which becomes machine instructions, which become billions of tiny switches flipping, which become a pattern of light on a screen. Then you press save and it travels to the other side of the planet in a fifth of a second.

    Nobody holds all of that in their head at once. Engineers manage it with abstraction: every layer offers a small, simple promise to the layer above, and hides how it keeps that promise. A web page doesn't know about transistors. A transistor doesn't know about web pages. This module climbs the tower one floor at a time, from the bottom.

    Why should an artist care? Because the layers leak. Why is a loop over pixels[] slow but a shader fast? Why does loadImage() have to go in preload()? Why does 255 + 1 sometimes give 0? Why does your sketch look different on a phone? Every one of those answers lives on a floor below the one you are working on. And many artists work on purpose with those leaks: glitch art, data bending, net art, live coding.

    How to use this: each chapter has one or two 3D scenes. Drag to turn them. Click whatever glows. Read the text underneath. Break things on purpose.

    chapter 1

    Everything is switches

    A computer can only store one kind of thing: switches that are either on or off. Every photo, song, word and sketch you have ever made is, deep down, a very long row of on and off.

    One switch is a bit (binary digit): 0 or 1. On its own a bit can't say much: yes or no, black or white. But line up eight of them and you get 256 different patterns (2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2). Eight bits is a byte.

    To read a byte as a number, give each position a value, doubling from right to left: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128. Add up the values of the switches that are on. 01000001 is 64 + 1 = 65. That's all binary is: counting where each column is worth twice the one to its right, instead of ten times.

    Then we just agree on what the numbers mean. Since 1963 we have agreed (the ASCII code) that 65 means the letter A, 66 means B, 32 is a space. Today's Unicode extends the same idea to over 150,000 characters: ಕ, अ, 字, 🙂. And a colour on your screen is three bytes, one each for red, green and blue light.

    how manycalledenough for
    1 bitbiton / off, yes / no
    8 bitsbytea number 0–255, one letter, one channel of one pixel
    1,000 byteskilobyte (KB)a page of plain text
    1,000,000 bytesmegabyte (MB)one photo (compressed), a minute of music
    1,000,000,000 bytesgigabyte (GB)a film; what your laptop's RAM holds 8–32 of

    fill(83, 77, 73) is three bytes. loadPixels() gives you an array called pixels where every pixel is four numbers in a row (red, green, blue, alpha), each 0–255, because each is one byte. A 400 × 400 canvas is 640,000 bytes. Draw it at pixelDensity(2) and it is four times that.

    • Type your initials. What colour is your name?
    • Press count in binary and watch the rightmost bulb. It flips every time. The next one flips half as often. Then what happens after 255?
    • Make pure yellow. Which switches did you need? Why is yellow "red + green" on a screen when it isn't in paint?
    chapter 2

    A switch made of sand

    A bit needs a switch, and a computer needs billions of them, flipping billions of times a second, without anyone's finger. The answer is the transistor: a switch flipped by electricity.

    Silicon, the main ingredient of sand, is a semiconductor: it conducts electricity badly, but you can change how badly. Add a pinch of certain other atoms (this is called doping) and you get regions that are full of free electrons. A transistor puts two of those regions, the source and the drain, side by side with plain silicon between them. Nothing can flow across the gap.

    On top of the gap sits a gate, separated from the silicon by a layer of glass so thin it is a few dozen atoms thick. Put a voltage on the gate and its charge pulls electrons up underneath the glass, forming a thin conducting channel. Now current flows from source to drain. Take the voltage away and the channel vanishes. On, off. 1, 0.

    The idea that matters: a transistor is a switch whose input is the same kind of thing as its output (a voltage). So the output of one can flip the next one, which can flip the next. That is what makes it possible to build anything at all out of them.

    How small, how many

    1945ENIAC, one of the first electronic computers, uses 17,468 vacuum tubes as switches: glass bulbs, hot, power-hungry, burning out daily. It fills a room.
    1947Bardeen, Brattain and Shockley build the first transistor at Bell Labs. It is the size of your thumb.
    1958–59Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce put several transistors and their wires on one piece of material: the integrated circuit, or chip.
    1971The Intel 4004, the first commercial microprocessor: about 2,300 transistors.
    1965–nowGordon Moore notices the number of transistors on a chip doubles roughly every two years. It keeps happening for half a century ("Moore's law").
    todayA laptop processor has tens of billions of transistors (Apple's M3 Max: 92 billion), each a few dozen nanometres across. A human hair is about 80,000 nm wide.

    They are made by photolithography: shining ultraviolet light through a stencil onto a polished disc of pure silicon, then etching, doping and layering, dozens of times. In other words: a chip is printed. It's printmaking, at the scale of atoms.

    • Find the voltage where the lamp just turns on. Why do you think chips are designed to never sit near that point?
    • Notice no electrons ever go into the gate. Why is that useful, if the gate is driven by another transistor's output?
    chapter 3

    Switches that decide

    Put two switches in a row and the light comes on only if both are on. Put them side by side and either one will do. You've just built logic.

    Two switches in series (one after the other): current gets through only if A and B are closed. That's an AND gate. Two switches in parallel (two paths): current gets through if A or B is closed. That's an OR gate. A switch wired so that it pulls the output down when it's on gives you the opposite of its input: a NOT gate.

    That's it. In 1854 George Boole wrote down an algebra of true and false using only AND, OR and NOT. In 1937 a 21-year-old student called Claude Shannon noticed that electrical switching circuits obey exactly Boole's algebra. Every chip since is built on that observation.

    A surprising fact: you only need one kind of gate. NAND (AND followed by NOT) can be wired up to make NOT, AND, OR, XOR, and therefore everything. Same for NOR: the computer that guided Apollo 11 to the Moon was built from about 2,800 chips that each held two NOR gates, and nothing else.

    Every if you write is a gate. if (mouseIsPressed && key === 'a') is an AND. || is OR. ! is NOT. And JavaScript even has XOR for whole numbers: 5 ^ 3 is 6, because it XORs every bit (101 ^ 011 = 110).

    • Pick XOR. Find the two rows where it differs from OR. Say in words what XOR means.
    • On paper: if you only had NAND gates, how would you make a NOT? (Hint: what if both inputs are the same wire?)
    chapter 4

    Arithmetic out of logic

    Gates make decisions. Arrange them the right way and those decisions are arithmetic. This is the moment a pile of switches becomes a calculator.

    Add two one-bit numbers and there are only four cases:

    ABA + Bsum digitcarry
    00000
    01110
    10110
    112 = 10 in binary01

    Look at the last two columns. The sum digit is exactly XOR. The carry is exactly AND. One XOR and one AND make a half adder. Two half adders and an OR also take a carry coming in from the right: a full adder. Chain four full adders, each one's carry feeding the next, and you can add any two numbers from 0 to 15. Chain 64 and you have the adder inside your laptop.

    Speed is a wiring problem. Each gate takes a tiny time to settle (picoseconds, in a modern chip). In a ripple adder, the leftmost digit has to wait for all the others. That waiting, added up across a whole chip, is what sets how fast the clock in the next chapter can tick. Real CPUs use cleverer "carry-lookahead" adders that guess the carries in parallel.

    • Press 15 + 1 and count how many boxes go yellow one after the other.
    • Make 9 + 9. The answer is 18, which doesn't fit in 4 bits. Where does the 16 go? This is called overflow, and it's why 255 + 1 = 0 in a byte.
    • Subtraction is addition in disguise: computers subtract by adding a flipped number plus one. Look up "two's complement".
    chapter 5

    Remembering, on the beat

    An adder forgets its answer the moment its inputs change. To compute anything in steps you need two more ideas: a circuit that remembers, and a drumbeat that tells everything when to move.

    Memory from feedback. Take two NOR gates and wire each one's output back into the other's input. The pair settles into one of two stable states and stays there, even after you let go of the inputs, because each gate keeps the other one held. Pulse one input and it flips; pulse the other and it flips back. That loop is a latch: one bit of memory, made of nothing but gates. Put eight side by side and you can hold a byte. Give each byte a number, its address, and you have memory.

    (Your laptop's main memory, DRAM, cheats for density: each bit is one transistor plus a tiny capacitor holding a charge, which leaks and has to be topped up thousands of times a second. Same idea, different trick.)

    The clock. Signals take different times to ripple through different paths, as you saw in the adder. If every part changed whenever it felt like it, the whole machine would be reading half-finished answers. So there is a clock: a signal that goes up and down, up and down, and every memory cell only takes in a new value on the moment it goes up (the "rising edge"). Between ticks, everything settles. On the tick, everything moves one step, together.

    How fast is the drumbeat? A 3 GHz processor ticks three billion times a second. In one tick, light travels about 10 centimetres. At that speed the size of the chip, and the distance to memory, starts to matter. That's chapter 7.

    p5's draw() is a clock: it ticks about 60 times a second, and frameCount counts the ticks. Every variable you make with let is a few bytes at some address in memory, which is why it still holds its value the next time draw() runs.

    • Write your own initials into addresses 0 to 2 (use the data slider to find the letter codes: A is 65).
    • Stop the clock. Change the address and data a few times. Did anything get written? Now tick once.
    chapter 6

    Fetch, decode, execute

    You have an adder, memory and a clock. Here is the idea that turns them into a computer: store the instructions in the same memory as the data, and have a little machine read them one at a time.

    In 1945 John von Neumann wrote up a design (for a machine called EDVAC) with this shape, and almost every computer since has followed it. The CPU (central processing unit) has a few special memory cells of its own, called registers:

    • PC, the program counter: the address of the next instruction.
    • IR, the instruction register: the instruction it's working on.
    • A, the accumulator: a scratchpad for the number being worked on.

    And it does one thing, forever:

    1. Fetch: read the byte at address PC into IR. Add 1 to PC.
    2. Decode: split the byte. The first 4 bits say what to do (the opcode); the last 4 say where (an address).
    3. Execute: do it. Maybe that means using the ALU (arithmetic logic unit: chapter 4's adder, plus friends).

    Below is a complete working CPU with 16 bytes of memory and 8 instructions. It's a toy, but not a lie: it really runs, and everything your laptop does is this loop with more registers, more instructions and more speed.

    opcodeinstructionwhat it does
    0000HALTstop
    0001LOAD aA = memory[a]
    0010ADD aA = A + memory[a]
    0011SUB aA = A − memory[a]
    0100STORE amemory[a] = A
    0101JUMP aPC = a (the next instruction comes from a)
    0110JZ aif A is 0, PC = a (a decision!)
    0111OUTshow A

    Only two of those instructions are really interesting. JUMP lets a program repeat itself: that's every loop. JZ lets it choose: that's every if. With arithmetic, memory, a loop and a choice, a machine can compute anything any computer can compute (given enough memory and time). Alan Turing proved that in 1936, before anyone had built one.

    Your p5 code is turned into instructions like these by the browser's JavaScript engine, a few milliseconds after the page loads. A single x = x + 1 is roughly LOAD, ADD, STORE. A for loop is a SUB, a JZ and a JUMP. A real CPU knows over a thousand instructions and runs several of them at once per tick, but the loop is the same.

    • Run count by 3 at high speed. What happens after 255? (Chapter 4's overflow, again.)
    • Open the editor and change the 3 at address 14 to a 5. Reload and run.
    • Harder: make the countdown start at 9 and count down by 3. When does it stop? Why does it not stop if you start at 10?
    • Hardest: write a program that STOREs into its own code and changes what it does next.
    chapter 7

    Everything on one board

    Zoom out from the CPU and you find a city: memory, storage, graphics, network, power, all wired together. The main fact about this city is that distance is time.

    The toy CPU had all its memory right next to it. A real one has a few dozen registers inside, then several layers of cache (small, fast memory on the same chip), then RAM on separate chips a few centimetres away, then storage (an SSD) that keeps your files when the power is off, then the whole internet beyond that.

    The parts are not just in different places; they are wildly different speeds. Every step down this ladder holds more and is slower, often by ten or a hundred times. This is called the memory hierarchy, and it is the single most useful thing to know about why software is fast or slow.

    Caching is everywhere. Because far is slow, every layer keeps copies of things it might need again close by: the CPU caches RAM, RAM caches the disk, your browser caches websites, DNS caches addresses (chapter 10). When something is mysteriously fast the second time, a cache is usually why.

    loadImage() goes in preload() because the file may come from the slowest tower in that picture, and p5 wants it ready before setup(). Looping over every pixel in JavaScript runs on the CPU, a few at a time; a shader runs the same little program on the GPU's thousands of cores, one per pixel, all at once. That is why shaders can do per-pixel effects at 60 frames per second that would crawl in a for loop.

    chapter 8

    The program in charge

    Your laptop is playing music, downloading a file, running a browser with forty tabs and your p5 sketch, all at once, on a handful of cores. It isn't, really. It's taking turns, very fast.

    The operating system (macOS, Windows, Linux, Android, iOS) is the first program that runs when you switch on, and it never stops. Its core, the kernel, is the only program allowed to touch the hardware directly. Everything else has to ask it. It has four main jobs:

    • Share the CPU. Each running program is a process. The kernel's scheduler gives each one a short time slice on a core (a few milliseconds), then freezes it, saves its registers, and loads the next one. This is a context switch.
    • Share memory. Each process gets its own private range of addresses and cannot read anyone else's. (When one tries, that's a crash, not a hack.)
    • Files. Turning "a few billion flash cells" into folders with names.
    • Devices. Small programs called drivers translate between the kernel and each piece of hardware: keyboard, screen, Wi-Fi, camera.

    It's animation. A film is still frames shown fast enough that you see motion. Multitasking is programs taking turns fast enough that you see them all running. Same trick, same reason it works: you're slow.

    • Set 1 core and add five more programs. Does anything ever stop getting a turn?
    • Make the time slice very short. More switching means more fair, but each switch costs a little time. Where would you set it?
    • On your own laptop: open Activity Monitor (Mac) or Task Manager (Windows). Count the processes. It's usually several hundred.
    chapter 9

    Packets, and the paths they take

    The internet is not a set of cables between you and a website. It's tens of thousands of networks agreeing to pass each other's envelopes along, without anyone in charge of the whole route.

    When your laptop sends anything, it chops it into small packets (usually up to about 1,500 bytes). Each packet gets a label on the front: where it's from, where it's going (an IP address, like 203.0.113.7), and its number in the sequence. Then it's handed to the nearest router.

    A router is a computer whose whole job is to look at the address on each packet and pass it to a neighbour that is closer to the destination. It doesn't know the full route. Nobody does. Each packet might go a different way, depending on which links are busy or broken right now. They arrive out of order; some never arrive at all.

    That mess is tidied up at the two ends by TCP: the receiver puts the packets back in order using their numbers and sends back acknowledgements; the sender re-sends anything that isn't acknowledged in time. The network in the middle is allowed to be unreliable, because the ends make it reliable.

    Designed to survive. ARPANET, the internet's ancestor (1969), was built from the idea that a network with no centre and many paths keeps working when parts of it fail. Break half the routers above: as long as some path exists, the message still arrives. The modern internet still runs on the TCP/IP rules switched on across ARPANET on 1 January 1983.

    • Set packet loss to 30% and send again. The message still arrives complete. What did it cost?
    • Break routers until you cut the network in two. What happens to the packets?
    • Under the sea: almost all traffic between continents travels on about 500 undersea fibre-optic cables. Look up the cables landing in Mumbai and Chennai.
    chapter 10

    What happens when you press Enter

    Between typing an address and seeing the page, your browser holds a short conversation with four or five computers you have never heard of, in four different languages. All of it in about half a second.

    Each "language" is a protocol: a set of rules both sides agree on, like the format of a letter. They stack, just like the layers of the machine:

    protocolits jobanalogy
    DNSturn a name (www.example.com) into an IP addresslooking up a phone number
    IPget one packet to one address, best effortthe postal system
    TCPa reliable, ordered stream on top of unreliable packetsnumbered pages, and a receipt for each
    TLSencrypt the stream and prove who the server isa sealed envelope with an ID card inside
    HTTPask for a file, get a filethe letter itself: "please send me /index.html"

    The web is text. Under the encryption, an HTTP request is a few lines of plain English-ish text, and a web page is a text file of HTML. Tim Berners-Lee, at CERN in 1989–91, invented three things: HTML (the format), HTTP (the protocol) and the URL (the address). That's the whole web. Everything else is the internet that already existed underneath it.

    loadJSON(url), loadImage(url) and JavaScript's fetch(url) each kick off this exact conversation. That's why they are asynchronous: your sketch can't just stop and wait 400 ms at 60 frames a second, so it keeps drawing and you get the data a few frames later, in a callback or preload().

    • On any page, open your browser's developer tools (⌥⌘I on Mac, F12 on Windows), click Network and reload. Every row is one HTTP request. How many does this page need? How many does Instagram?
    • In a terminal, type nslookup example.com to do the DNS step yourself.
    chapter 11

    From text to pixels

    The server sent back a text file. The browser has to turn it into a picture: by building a tree, turning the tree into boxes, and painting the boxes. Then it does it again every time anything changes.

    1. Parse. The browser reads the HTML and builds the DOM (document object model): a tree in which every tag is a node and tags inside tags are its children.
    2. Style. It reads the CSS and works out every property of every node: colour, font, size, margin.
    3. Layout. It works out where every box goes. Every element is a rectangle, nested inside its parent's rectangle.
    4. Paint. It draws the boxes, back to front, into layers.
    5. Composite. The GPU stacks the layers into the final frame on your screen.

    Meanwhile JavaScript can reach into the tree at any time (document.querySelector(...)) and change it, and the browser redoes whatever steps it has to.

    That page was a toy. The one you're reading isn't. Below, the browser hands us the position and size of every element in this chapter, and we rebuild it in 3D: the higher a box floats, the deeper it is nested in the tree.

    createCanvas(400, 400) adds one <canvas> element to the DOM, one box among the others. Everything you draw is pixels inside that one box; the browser lays it out and composites it like any other. createButton() and createSlider() add real DOM elements beside it, which is why they look like web page parts and not like your drawing.

    • Right-click anything on this page and choose Inspect. That's the DOM tree, live. Change some text in it and watch the page change.
    • Switch the toy page to the tree view. Which elements are leaves (no children)? Those are the ones with something to show.

    All the way down

    You've climbed from one switch to a web page. Here is the whole tower on one screen, some numbers worth remembering, and where to go deeper.

    layerthe one idea
    1 · bitseverything is a pattern of on/off; meaning is an agreement
    2 · transistorsa switch flipped by voltage, whose output can flip the next
    3 · gatesswitches in series = AND, in parallel = OR; NAND makes everything
    4 · adderssum = XOR, carry = AND; arithmetic is logic
    5 · memory & clockfeedback remembers; the clock makes everything step together
    6 · CPUfetch, decode, execute, forever; code and data share memory
    7 · the machinefar is slow; so everything caches
    8 · OSprograms take turns so fast it looks simultaneous
    9 · networkspackets find their own way; the ends make it reliable
    10 · the webDNS → TCP → TLS → HTTP: a conversation in text
    11 · the browsertext → tree → boxes → pixels, 60 times a second
