Chapter 1 · c. 300 million BCE – 1971

Before computers

A generative work is one where the artist builds a system and the system makes the picture. Nature has been doing this forever. People have been doing it for at least a thousand years. The computer only made the system fast.

NATURE

The first generative systems were not made by anyone

Look at a sunflower head. Nobody arranged those seeds. Each new seed simply appears at the centre, turned 137.5° from the last one, and pushes the older seeds outward. That single rule — one angle, repeated — produces the spirals. Change the angle by half a degree and the spirals fall apart. This is phyllotaxis, and the 137.5° is the golden angle, the one that packs seeds most tightly.

Drag the slider. The dots are placed by exactly the formula above (Vogel, 1979: r = c√n, θ = n × 137.5°). The flower is a program.

Zebra stripes, leopard spots, the lattice on a giraffe. In 1952 Alan Turing — yes, that Turing — published The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis, proposing that two chemicals spreading through skin at different speeds, one feeding the other and one killing it, would settle into stripes or spots on their own. He was right; biologists confirmed it decades later. Below is the Gray–Scott version of his equations. Two numbers, f and k, decide whether you get coral, spots, worms or nothing.

The textile cone snail, Conus textile, carries on its shell a pattern that looks like a computation — because it is. Each new row of shell is laid down by cells that look only at their neighbours in the previous row. Stephen Wolfram formalised this as elementary cellular automata in 1983. Rule 30, on the shell and on the screen, is the same eight-line lookup table.

c. 3000 BCE →

Kolam: a drawing you do not compose, you execute

Before dawn, outside doors across Tamil Nadu, a grid of dots is laid in rice flour. A single line is then drawn around the dots — never through them, never lifting — until it closes on itself. The drawer is not deciding what the picture looks like; she is following a rule, and the picture is what the rule produces. Mathematicians and computer scientists (Gift Siromoney at Madras Christian College, from the 1970s) have described kolam with formal grammars, the same tools used to describe programming languages.

The sketch below cheats a little. It uses the "tile" trick — every cell between four dots gets one of two quarter-arc tiles — which guarantees the curves join into closed loops. That trick was rediscovered independently by Sébastien Truchet in 1704 and by Cyril Stanley Smith in 1987. The kolam makers knew it first.

c. 900 →

Islamic geometric pattern: a grammar of stars

From Córdoba to Isfahan to Fatehpur Sikri, craftsmen covered walls with patterns that never repeat their motif in the wrong place and never leave a gap. They did it with compass and straightedge and a small set of rules: a grid, a way of dividing the circle, a way of connecting the divisions. The girih tile system (five tiles, decorated with lines, assembled edge to edge) produced patterns so intricate that in 2007 physicists showed some 15th-century examples are quasi-crystalline — a structure Western mathematics only described in 1974.

The artisan's genius was in the system. Any two artisans with the same rules would make the same wall. That is the definition we are working towards.

1787

Mozart's dice game

Musikalisches Würfelspiel. A table of 176 pre-written bars of music. Throw two dice sixteen times; each throw picks one bar from a column; the result is a sixteen-bar waltz that has almost certainly never been heard before. There are 1116 ≈ 45 quadrillion possible waltzes. Mozart (or a publisher using his name — attribution is disputed) composed none of them and all of them. The possibility space is the work.

1913–1951

Chance enters the studio

The modernists let go of the hand on purpose.

  • Marcel Duchamp, 3 Standard Stoppages (1913–14). Drop a metre-long thread from a metre's height three times; fix each where it lands; make rulers from the curves. A new unit of measurement, generated by gravity.
  • Jean (Hans) Arp, Collage with Squares Arranged According to the Laws of Chance (1916–17). Tear paper, let it fall, glue it where it lies. Dada's protest against reason, and one of the first pictures whose composition is a procedure.
  • John Cage, Music of Changes (1951). Every note, duration and dynamic chosen by tossing coins and consulting the I Ching. Cage: "I have nothing to say and I am saying it."
  • Ellsworth Kelly, Spectrum Colors Arranged by Chance (1951–53). A grid of numbered squares; colours drawn from a hat.
  • Fluxus event scores (1960s). George Brecht's Drip Music: "A source of dripping water and an empty vessel are arranged so that the water falls into the vessel." Yoko Ono's Grapefruit (1964): "Imagine the clouds dripping. Dig a hole in your garden to put them in." The instruction is the piece; the performance is one output.

1961

A hundred thousand billion poems

Raymond Queneau's Cent mille milliards de poèmes: ten sonnets printed on strips, so any line can be combined with any other. 1014 poems in one thin book. Queneau's group, the Oulipo, made rules the whole point: Georges Perec later wrote a 300-page novel without the letter e. Constraint as engine.

1959–1961

Drawing machines

Jean Tinguely's Méta-Matics (1959): kinetic sculptures that held a felt pen and scribbled abstract drawings, which visitors could buy. Tinguely entered them in an art competition; they won. Desmond Paul Henry (1961) rebuilt a WWII bombsight computer to draw with; nobody, including Henry, could predict its curves. The machine was not yet digital, but the question was already the modern one: who is the artist?

1967–1971

Sol LeWitt writes the program

Sol LeWitt (1928–2007) is the bridge. His wall drawings are not pictures; they are short paragraphs of instructions, sold as certificates, drawn on the wall by whoever the museum hires, and painted over when the show ends. Different draftspeople get different walls from the same instruction, and LeWitt said all of them were correct. He was writing code for humans.

In conceptual art the idea or concept is the most important aspect of the work. When an artist uses a conceptual form of art, it means that all of the planning and decisions are made beforehand and the execution is a perfunctory affair. The idea becomes a machine that makes the art.Sol LeWitt, "Paragraphs on Conceptual Art", 1967

What we take from all this

Three things, and they are the vocabulary for the rest of the course.

  • A rule, repeated, makes form. The sunflower's angle; the kolam's arc; ten thousand lines.
  • Chance is a material. Arp, Cage, LeWitt used it deliberately, in measured amounts. So will you — the number you give random() is a design decision.
  • The artist designs the possibility space, not the picture. Mozart's 45 quadrillion waltzes. Each output is one point in the space. The space is the work.

In 1965, in a small gallery in Stuttgart, somebody plugged all of this into a computer.
