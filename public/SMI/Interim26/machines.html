02

The machine

A pen plotter moves a real pen over paper, following paths in an SVG file. It draws lines, not pixels: no fills, no gradients, no transparency.

Sheet

A2

420 × 594 mm. Keep at least 15 mm margin on every side for clamping and tape.

Line

Strokes only

Every mark is a path the pen follows. Tone comes from density, hatching or repeated passes.

Colour

One pen per layer

Each colour is a separate layer, and the plot pauses for a pen change.

The plotter arrives in Week 2 and is demonstrated on Day 11. How long a full A2 plot takes is still being measured; until then, plan for a final plot of no more than two to three hours. To confirm on Day 11

03

Preparing the SVG

Most failed plots fail in the file. Optimise paths before the slot, not during it.

  1. Export from your system in millimetres.

    The document should be 420 × 594 mm (or your chosen smaller size), with the drawing inside the margins.

  2. Remove fills.

    Convert every shape to a stroke. Anything filled will either be ignored or drawn as an outline.

  3. Merge, sort and simplify paths.

    The free tool vpype does this in one line. Ask Codex to install it with you if it isn't there.

    vpype read outputs/production/final.svg \ linemerge --tolerance 0.1mm linesort reloop linesimplify \ write --page-size a2 --center outputs/production/final-plot.svg
  4. Look at it before you trust it.

    Open the optimised file next to the original. Check that no lines have disappeared and nothing moved outside the margins.

    vpype read outputs/production/final-plot.svg show
  5. Separate colours into layers.

    One layer per pen, in the order they'll be drawn: usually lightest first.

Prompt 16 in the prompt library asks Codex to do this preparation with you and report the path count and drawing length.

04

Pens and paper

Pens

Choose for the line

  • Fineliners (0.1–0.8 mm): even, archival lines.
  • Gel pens: bolder, can skip on fast moves.
  • Brush pens: line weight changes with speed and pressure.
  • Always test a new pen on your paper first.
Paper

Choose for the ink

  • Smooth, heavy paper (200–300 gsm) stays flat and keeps lines crisp.
  • Textured or absorbent paper softens lines and can bleed.
  • Dense overlapping lines can soak and tear thin paper.
  • Keep a sheet of the same paper for tests.

Pen and paper are part of the identity's position, not a finishing detail. Say why you chose them in your spec sheet.

05

The test plot

Day 13. Plot a small section, about 10 × 10 cm, of the densest part of your drawing, on your final paper with your final pens.

  • Time it. Multiply up to estimate the full plot. If it's longer than a slot, simplify.
  • Check the ink. Does dense hatching bleed, pool or tear the paper?
  • Check the line. Does the pen skip, drag or blot at the start of lines?
  • Photograph it for your test log in tests/, with a note on what you'll change.

When it goes wrong

ProblemLikely cause and fix
Lines are doubled or drawn twiceDuplicate paths in the SVG. Run linemerge and check for overlapping shapes in your code.
The pen draws between shapesPen height too low, or travel moves not lifting. Check the pen-up position before restarting.
The plot is taking far too longPaths aren't sorted, or there are too many tiny segments. Run linesort and linesimplify.
Ink pools or the paper cocklesToo many lines in one place. Reduce density, change pen, or use heavier paper.
Part of the drawing is missingFills that weren't converted to strokes, or shapes outside the page. Check the SVG before the slot.
09

Safety first

Uncured resin is a skin and eye irritantRead this section before you touch the printer, the vat, a fresh print or the wash station.
  • Nitrile gloves, always, whenever you handle the vat, the build plate, fresh prints or anything wet with resin. Latex isn't enough.
  • Eye protection when pouring resin, scraping the plate or removing supports.
  • Ventilation. Keep the room ventilated and the printer's lid closed while it runs.
  • Nothing down the sink. Resin and used solvent are cured in sunlight or UV, then disposed of as the studio instructs.
  • Skin contact: wash at once with soap and cold water, and tell the facilitator. Don't use solvent on skin.
10

The machine

A resin printer cures liquid resin with light, one thin layer at a time, on a plate that rises out of the vat. It's very precise, and it's slow in height, not in number of parts.

Size

Small and precise

Fine detail, but a small build volume. Build volume: confirm on Day 11

Time

Height sets the clock

Print time depends on the tallest part. Several small parts on one plate take no longer than one.

After

Wash and cure

Every print must be washed and UV-cured before it's safe and finished. Allow half a day.

11

Preparing the model

  1. Export an STL in millimetres.

    Check the dimensions in the slicer match what you intended.

  2. Make it watertight.

    The mesh must be closed, with no holes, flipped faces or non-manifold edges. Prompt 17 in the prompt library asks Codex to check and report.

  3. Give every part thickness.

    Walls of at least 1–1.5 mm, and no free-standing detail thinner than about 0.5 mm. Lines and surfaces from your system need to be swept or thickened into solids.

  4. Hollow large solids.

    Large solid parts waste resin and can crack. Hollow them, with wall thickness of about 2 mm and at least two drain holes so resin can escape.

  5. Orient and support in the slicer.

    Tilt the part about 30–45° from the plate, so no large flat face prints parallel to it. Put supports where marks won't matter.

The numbers above are common starting points for resin printing; the test print is where you find the right ones for this printer and resin.

12

Wash, cure, finish

  1. Remove the plate with gloves on.

    Let excess resin drip back into the vat first.

  2. Wash.

    In isopropyl alcohol (or the wash solution the studio provides), following the station's timing. Don't over-soak; it can soften detail.

  3. Remove supports.

    While the part is still slightly soft, with flush cutters and eye protection.

  4. Dry, then cure.

    Under UV until the surface is no longer tacky. Over-curing makes parts brittle.

  5. Finish.

    Sand support marks lightly if they matter. Photograph the result for your test log.

When it goes wrong

ProblemLikely cause and fix
Nothing on the plateThe print didn't stick. Check the plate is clean and level; the first layers may need more exposure.
A part broke off mid-printToo few supports, or a large face parallel to the plate. Re-orient and add supports.
Holes or missing facesThe mesh isn't watertight. Repair it before slicing.
Cracks after curingTrapped resin in a solid or badly drained hollow part, or over-curing. Hollow with drain holes, cure less.
Detail is lostFeatures thinner than the printer can hold. Thicken them in your system, not in the slicer.
15

Why a print

An archival print uses pigment inks on archival paper, made to last. It can hold what a pen can't: tone, colour, fine density and millions of marks.

Choose it because the identity's work needs tone or colour, not only because it's quick. If you're printing as a fallback for a plot or a resin piece, prepare the file early so it's ready if needed. Framing and mounting are still being arranged. To be updated

The file

Render at full resolution from your system. Never enlarge a small image.

Final sizePixels at 300 PPI
A4 · 210 × 297 mm2480 × 3508
A3 · 297 × 420 mm3508 × 4961
A2 · 420 × 594 mm4961 × 7016
  • Format: TIFF, flattened, with the colour profile embedded.
  • Colour: work and export in the profile the facilitator gives you for the printer and paper. Soft-proof on screen before printing.
  • Vector work: an SVG can be rendered straight to 300 PPI; ask Codex to rasterise it at the exact pixel size (prompt 18 in the library).
  • Margins: leave a border of plain paper unless you've agreed a borderless print.

Maximum print width depends on the printer. Confirm on Day 11

17

Paper

Matte cotton rag

Soft and deep

No glare, a soft surface, rich tone. Blacks are less dense; very fine detail softens slightly.

Gloss or baryta

Sharp and dense

Deep blacks and crisp detail, with some sheen. Shows fingerprints and reflects light.

The paper is part of the identity's position. A record of loss on a glossy sheet reads differently from the same image on matte rag. Say why in your spec sheet.

18

Test strips and proofs

Days 12–13. Print before you print.

  1. Test strip.

    A narrow strip across the darkest, lightest and finest parts of the image, at full size, on the final paper.

  2. Look in daylight and under the exhibition light.

    Do shadows block up? Do fine lines hold? Is the colour what you saw on screen?

  3. Adjust in the system, not by hand.

    Change parameters or the colour mapping and re-render, so the final is still the system's output.

  4. Full proof, then final.

    A full-size proof once the strip is right; the final on Days 16–17.

19

Handling

  • Clean, dry hands, or cotton gloves. Hold prints by the edges.
  • Let it dry. Leave prints flat and uncovered for a day before stacking, sleeving or framing.
  • Store flat, with tissue between prints, away from sunlight and damp.
